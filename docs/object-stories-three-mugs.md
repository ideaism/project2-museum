# The Glitching Archive — Object Stories for Three Political Mugs

## 1. Constellation Thesis

These three mugs make politics small enough to hold, but not small enough to settle. Each is an everyday ceramic vessel: made for tea, cupboards, desks, sinks, and hands. Each also carries a public claim into private routine. The miners mug compresses energy, work, family pressure, and class conflict into a black printed image. The People's March for Jobs mug turns a route from Liverpool to London into a handled memory map. The Labour mug reduces a wider political movement into institutional name, colour, and brand.

Together they trace a movement from street mobilisation, to labour conflict, to party-political framing. That movement is not a clean timeline. Protest enters the kitchen. The kitchen sends memory back to the street. A party logo can invite belonging while flattening the disputes that made it possible.

Pouring is the right interaction because the archive is not only opened; it is tipped, strained, and made unstable. A slight tilt lets the catalogue surface appear. A deeper pour releases inference, feeling, and political atmosphere. Full inversion exposes what does not pour cleanly: missing owners, contested testimony, redacted harm, and visitor questions that refuse a final label.

## 2. Shared Narrative Rules

- Separate what is visible from what is known through research.
- Label every fragment before the text appears: fact, visible evidence, historical context, inference, speculation, visitor prompt, unresolved, redacted, or ethical caution.
- Do not make speculation sound like museum certainty.
- Keep unknown maker, provenance, donor, and use histories visible as unknown.
- Treat visitor annotations as local, situated contributions, not verified history.
- Use redaction to protect uncertainty, privacy, and the right to opacity, not to decorate absence.
- Let contradictory readings coexist in projection mode without forcing one correct ending.

## 3. Mug 01 — Support the Miners

### Object ID

miners-mug

### Display Title

Support the Miners: Coal, Justice, Future

### One-line Hook

A kitchen cup carrying coal, family, strike, and unresolved justice.

### Visible Evidence

- White glazed ceramic mug with a curved handle.
- Black printed illustration and lettering.
- Visible text: "BRITAIN'S COAL FOR BRITAIN'S FUTURE".
- Visible text: "SUPPORT THE MINERS".
- Visible text: "STRIKING FOR JUSTICE 1972 1974 1984".
- Motifs include a miner's face, three younger or family figures, and a mining structure.
- Maker, accession number, donor, and exact production context are not visible in the supplied image.

### Surface Layer — Official / Catalogue-like

- [visible evidence] White ceramic mug with black printed political design. Source: supplied object image.
- [visible evidence] The mug names three years: 1972, 1974, and 1984. Source: supplied object image.
- [fact] The mug's printed years align with documented national miners' strike contexts in 1972, 1974, and 1984-85. Source: University of Kent Special Collections, "History of Mining Strikes".
- [fact] The 1972 miners' strike ran from 9 January to 28 February 1972 and centred on a pay dispute. Source: University of Kent Special Collections, "History of Mining Strikes".
- [fact] The 1974 miners' strike led Edward Heath to call a general election; the miners later accepted a 35 percent pay offer. Source: University of Kent Special Collections.
- [fact] In March 1984 the National Coal Board announced plans to close 20 coal mines with 20,000 job losses. Source: Museum Wales / Big Pit article, "The Miners Strike of 1984".
- [research required] Exact maker, museum number, production run, sale context, and whether proceeds supported strike funds require collection verification.

### Middle Layer — Speculative / Felt Narrative

- [inference] The mug links coal to both national energy and the bodies that extracted it.
- [inference] "Future" appears as a political demand, not a neutral forecast.
- [speculation] The family-like faces may ask who carries the cost of a strike beyond the pit.
- [inference] Drinking from the cup could turn solidarity into a repeated domestic gesture.
- [speculation] The kitchen becomes a small picket line: warm, ordinary, and politically charged.
- [inference] The word "justice" frames the dispute as moral pressure, not only wages or closures.
- [speculation] The cup may have moved between support, fatigue, pride, anger, and storage.
- [inference] The black print makes the image feel closer to leaflet, banner, or strike poster than souvenir.

### Core Layer — Redacted / Unresolved

- [unresolved] Who first owned this mug, and what did supporting the miners mean in that room?
- [redacted] Names of families, donors, strike-breakers, or local disputes must not be invented.
- [ethical caution] Do not make one mining community stand in for all coalfields.
- [visitor prompt] Did this image feel like solidarity, pressure, propaganda, or memory to you?
- [unresolved] What futures were imagined here, and whose futures were already being closed?
- [redacted] Harm, arrests, debt, hunger, and family conflict need consent and evidence before display.
- [visitor prompt] What would you pour out: anger, pride, exhaustion, or a question?

### AR Microcopy

- Slight tilt prompt: Tip gently to read what the cup openly shows.
- Half pour prompt: Pour further to hear labour, family, and strike pressure.
- Full invert prompt: Turn it over for what the archive cannot safely settle.
- Annotation prompt: Leave a local question. It stays on this device.

### Sound Suggestions

- Surface sound: Soft ceramic clink, kettle breath, distant printed-label ticks.
- Middle sound: Low coal rumble, muffled marching feet, room-tone from a busy kitchen.
- Core sound: Dropouts, breath, near-silence, and fragments cut before becoming testimony.

### Visual / Glitch Suggestions

- Coal-dust speckling gathers around the printed dates as tilt increases.
- The mining structure briefly doubles like a misregistered screen print.
- "JUSTICE" holds steady while surrounding text slips out of alignment.
- Family faces fade behind redaction bars, then return as outlines.
- Projection mode casts the mug image as a black-and-white wall poster with missing edges.

### Ethics Note

The interface must not claim to speak for miners, their families, women in support groups, strike-breakers, police, unions, or coalfield communities. It can identify visible slogans and cite historical strike context, but it must not invent an owner, a family memory, a donation route, or a single community response. The 1984-85 strike remains painful, regionally varied, and politically contested. The core layer should keep uncertainty active: some names, harms, and loyalties may need to remain absent unless a collection record, consent process, or community partner makes them available.

## 4. Mug 02 — People's March for Jobs

### Object ID

jobs-march-mug

### Display Title

People's March for Jobs: Route Held in a Cup

### One-line Hook

A protest route becomes a handled map of unemployment and endurance.

### Visible Evidence

- Beige or speckled ceramic mug with a handle.
- Green typography and border.
- Visible text: "Peoples march for jobs".
- Visible text: "Liverpool-London May 1981".
- A printed route-map graphic crosses the mug.
- Many towns and cities are printed along the route; some are small and partially hard to read.
- Maker and ownership history are not visible in the supplied image.

### Surface Layer — Official / Catalogue-like

- [visible evidence] Ceramic mug with green text, route graphic, and town names. Source: supplied object image.
- [visible evidence] The mug identifies the event as "Peoples march for jobs" and "Liverpool-London May 1981". Source: supplied object image.
- [fact] V&A East Storehouse Lookup lists "Peoples March for Jobs", museum number C.106-1991, maker unknown, England, 1981, earthenware/firing. Source: V&A East Storehouse Lookup, "Marking moments in time".
- [fact] The 1981 People's March for Jobs was remembered by People's History Museum as a march from Liverpool to London. Source: People's History Museum Q&A with Dr Greig Campbell.
- [fact] PHM reports approximately 500 permanent marchers, split across western and eastern legs, with numbers swelling through supporters. Source: People's History Museum Q&A.
- [fact] The TUC Library states the Liverpool march left on 1 May 1981 and reached London on 31 May. Source: TUC Library Collections Blog.
- [fact] Hackney Museum records a related 1981 "Peoples march for jobs" badge and connects the march to unemployment, youth participants, and a month-long protest. Source: Hackney Museum object 2024.39.
- [research required] Confirm whether the supplied mug image is the same object as V&A C.106-1991 before using museum number in the live interface.

### Middle Layer — Speculative / Felt Narrative

- [inference] The route turns unemployment into geography: job loss is mapped across towns, not hidden in statistics.
- [inference] Walking becomes evidence. The body measures the distance between political promise and work.
- [speculation] Each printed place name may act like a stop, a host, a memory, or a blister.
- [inference] The green route line makes protest look planned, but the experience was lived step by step.
- [speculation] The mug may have held tea after the march, turning public endurance into private aftercare.
- [inference] A ceramic map can keep movement still without making it silent.
- [speculation] The towns included on the mug also raise questions about who was left off the route.
- [inference] Compared with the miners mug, this object foregrounds movement through national space rather than one industrial symbol.

### Core Layer — Redacted / Unresolved

- [unresolved] Which marchers, hosts, and local supporters are missing from this printed route?
- [visitor prompt] Where does unemployment sit on your own map: street, family, school, station, home?
- [ethical caution] Do not turn marchers into symbols of resilience while ignoring precarity and exhaustion.
- [redacted] Personal oral histories require permission; the mug cannot supply them by itself.
- [unresolved] Did this object commemorate victory, survival, disappointment, or the need to keep walking?
- [visitor prompt] Add a place name that belongs to this history but is not printed here.
- [redacted] Names of unemployed participants stay absent unless verified by records or consent.

- Half pour prompt: Pour to follow the march through towns, feet, and joblessness.
- Full invert prompt: Turn it over for the places and people the map cannot hold.

### Sound Suggestions

- Surface sound: Pencil tracing a map, ceramic scrape, light paper rustle.
- Middle sound: Layered footsteps, breath, passing traffic, hall ambience.
- Core sound: Fading place names, interrupted roll call, low room tone.

### Visual / Glitch Suggestions

- Town names pulse as if called out on a projection wall.
- Unreadable small text becomes magnified but not fully resolved.
- Footstep marks appear, then dissolve into local visitor annotations.

### Ethics Note

The interface must not flatten unemployment into a heroic walking story. The march can be contextualised through cited public-history sources, but individual motivations, hardship, and political disagreements cannot be assumed from the mug alone. Do not claim the printed route includes everyone who mattered. Do not treat a town name as a complete local history. Visitor annotations should invite place-based memory and dispute while remaining clearly separate from verified records. The core layer should preserve the gap between route, experience, and archive.

## 6. Comparative Interaction Script

The visitor scans the miners mug first. A slight tilt reveals dates and printed slogans; a deeper pour releases coal dust, strike memory, family pressure, and the word "justice" refusing to sit still. At full inversion, the interface withholds invented testimony and asks what cannot be safely claimed.

They move to the People's March for Jobs mug. The phone follows the green route line. Town names become steps, stops, and questions. On the projection wall, the map stretches beyond the ceramic curve and leaves gaps for places not printed.

The Labour mug appears last. It is simpler, quieter, almost too legible: one red word on white ceramic. The visitor pours again and sees movement memory compressed into brand recognition. Fragments from the first two mugs interrupt the logo.

In projection mode, all three cups gather as a shadow archive: coal, route, party name; kitchen, street, institution; fact, inference, redaction. The visitor leaves one local question: "What work is missing from this cup?"
